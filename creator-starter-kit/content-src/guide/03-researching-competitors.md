# Researching competitors

Competitors are not a reason to quit. They prove that people spend money on this kind of solution. Your job is not to out-shout them but to understand what they do well and where customers are still underserved.

## Find your competitors

There are three types:

- **Direct:** sell a similar product to the same audience.
- **Indirect:** solve the same problem differently (a book, a course, a free video series).
- **Substitutes:** the audience's current workaround (a messy spreadsheet, doing nothing).

Search on marketplaces, social platforms and search engines using your audience's words. Collect 5 to 10 examples.

## What to record

Create a simple table:

| Competitor | Product & format | Price | Promise | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Also note how they present themselves: tone, visuals, how much is free, what they say about guarantees and support.

## Read reviews like a researcher

Reviews are free market research. Read the 5-star reviews to learn what customers love and the 3-star reviews to learn what is missing. Highlight repeated phrases. If several reviews say "too long" or "not beginner-friendly", you have found an opportunity.

## Find your gap

Look for angles where competitors are weak:

- **Audience:** they serve experts; you serve beginners.
- **Format:** they offer long courses; you offer quick checklists.
- **Experience:** their products are hard to use; yours is simple.
- **Price or scope:** they are expensive; you offer a smaller starter option.
- **Clarity:** they use jargon; you use plain language.

Your positioning statement can follow: "Unlike [competitor type], my product is [difference] for [audience]."

## Stay ethical

Study, don't copy. Never reuse someone else's text, designs or paid content. Being inspired by structure is fine; duplicating is not, and it damages your brand.

## Exercise

Analyse three competitors with the table, list the three most common complaints, and write your positioning statement.
